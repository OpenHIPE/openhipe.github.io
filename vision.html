Recent advances in artificial intelligence have significantly expanded the capabilities of machine perception, enabling computing systems to understand and interact with the physical world in increasingly sophisticated ways. As a result, a rapidly growing class of intelligent computing platforms is emerging, capable of perceiving, reasoning about, and interacting with both their surrounding environments and human users. This paradigm builds on interactive perception, in which sensing and action are coupled so that interaction itself generates information for subsequent reasoning and behavior [1]. For example, augmented-reality systems can help warehouse workers understand and coordinate with mobile robots while maintaining awareness of task-relevant physical objects [2, 3], while body-tracked virtual reality can translate patients’ movements into adaptive and measurable rehabilitation experiences [4].

Interactive Perception System​

Unlike traditional stationary machine perception systems, these emerging systems can leverage device mobility to interactively perceive their surroundings and continuously adapt to changing environments [5, 6]. We refer to these systems as interactive perception systems. Over the past decade, research has increasingly explored how to incorporate human perceptual behavior into the development of these intelligent systems to provide valuable guidance [7]. For example, XR systems can leverage human visual focus patterns to optimize system resource allocation [8, 9, 10], while robots can learn from demonstrations by human experts to perform complex tasks [11, 12].

Yet, the success of interactive perception systems cannot be established through algorithm-centric benchmarks alone [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Because these systems operate in a closed loop with people, human needs, capabilities, and behaviors must be treated as first-class considerations throughout their design, testing, validation, and improvement [18, 19, 20]. Evaluation must therefore examine not only the accuracy of individual perception algorithms, but also how perception errors affect human behavior [21], how people adapt to system behavior [22, 23, 24, 25, 26], and whether the resulting interactions remain effective, usable, safe, and robust across users, tasks, and environments [27, 28, 29].

Human-centered Evaluation​

Conducting such human-centered evaluations rigorously is challenging. Human behavior varies across individuals and over time, introducing experimental noise and complicating reproducibility [30, 31]. Moreover, observed performance often emerges from complex interactions among algorithms, devices, users, tasks, and physical environments, making it difficult to isolate failures and compare results across studies [32, 17, 1, 16]. Addressing these challenges requires researchers from machine learning, computing systems, human-centered computing, and application domains to develop shared evaluation methods and work across traditional disciplinary boundaries [33].

At present, however, these communities lack a coherent open ecosystem for conducting and sharing human-centered evaluations. Existing software is highly fragmented: sensor-data capture on XR and robotic hardware typically depends on product- or vendor-specific technologies [34, 35], captured data lacks standardized and reusable representations [36, 37, 38], and experimental workflows are often tied to particular devices or research prototypes [39]. Consequently, studies are difficult to reproduce, compare, or extend across platforms and institutions [33]. Moreover, without a supporting community and shared governance, individual research artifacts can easily become isolated efforts with limited adoption and long-term impact. The missing infrastructure is therefore both technical and social: researchers need open-source software to (1) capture and replay multimodal device data, (2) conduct reproducible experiments across systems and settings [37, 40], and (3) share knowledge, coordinate development, and establish evaluation practices as a community [41].

OpenHIPE​

To address this gap, we introduce OpenHIPE [42], an Open-Source Ecosystem for Human-Centered Interactive Perception System Evaluation, which is built upon our years of open-source projects [35, 13, 43, 39, 44, 45] in XR and robotics domains. Figure 1 shows an overview of the OpenHIPE ecosystem that will integrate high-performance sensor-data capture and replay toolkits, reproducible experimentation platforms, and an online community for sharing protocols, discussing results, and collaboratively evolving the ecosystem.

Our initial focus on XR and robotics will connect researchers across HCI, mobile computing, computer vision, computer graphics, and related domains to collaborate on the emerging interactive perception systems. We will also work closely with our academic and industry collaborators on ecosystem discovery and management activities. To promote sustained participation and external contributions, OpenHIPE will also adopt community-driven governance exemplified by successful open-source software organizations, such as ROS [46], Hugging Face [47], and MLCommons [48].
